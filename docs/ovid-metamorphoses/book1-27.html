Notes on the passage

The destruction of Argus is the mechanical center of the Io episode: everything before it has been preparation, everything after will flow from it. Mercury strikes while all hundred eyes are sleeping, having guided the last of them to rest with the unfinished tale of Syrinx. The act itself receives almost no narrative space — the elaborate ruse required to make it possible receives far more attention than the stroke that executes it.

Juno collects Argus’s eyes and sets them in the peacock’s tail, where they survive as decoration after ceasing to function as organs of sight. The image is characteristic of Ovid’s transformations: the original function is lost but something of the form persists. The guardian’s watchfulness, which filled dozens of lines, is now a pattern on a bird’s feathers.

Juno’s revenge on Io through the gadfly is harder to read. Io has been Jupiter’s victim throughout; now she is Juno’s as well. The punishment pursues her because she cannot be punished directly — she is innocent — but Juno’s jealousy requires an outlet, and Io is there. Her wandering under the gadfly’s torment covers the world: the Ionian Sea (named for her), the Bosporus (“cow-ford”), the Caucasus, Asia, Egypt. The catalog of her journey is both geographical and emotional — driven without purpose or rest, consciousness intact inside an animal body.

In Egypt, at the Nile, Jupiter persuades Juno to relent. He swears he will ask nothing more of Io. Io is restored. The reversal of the transformation happens carefully and with the same clinical attention as the original change. Speech returns last. Io speaks — cautiously, afraid of what sound will come — and what comes is human language. The episode ends.

Classical

The pronunciation of educated Romans of the late Republic and early Empire, reconstructed from ancient grammarians, spelling, loanwords, and verse. It is the system taught in most schools and universities today, and the one Ovid would have recognised.

Vowels. Each vowel is short or long, and length is part of the word: malum “evil” is not mālum “apple”. The popovers take length from the macrons of the dictionary form; an unmarked vowel is short.

Diphthongs. ae as in aisle (ai); au as in house (ow); oe as in boil (oy); eu is e gliding to u; ui in cui, huic is one syllable.

Consonants mostly as in English, with these fixed values:

  • c is always k: Cicerō = KIH-keh-roh. g is always hard: genus = GEH-noos.
  • v (consonantal u) is English w: virgō = WIHR-goh.
  • i before a vowel at the start of a word, and between vowels, is English y: iam, iuvenis. Between vowels it is doubled: maior = MAI-yor.
  • qu is kw; gu after n is gw: lingua = LIHNG-gwah.
  • s is always the s of sing, never z. t is always t: grātia = GRAH-tee-ah.
  • ph, th, ch are p, t, k with a puff of breath, not f, th, ch.
  • gn is ngn: magnus = MAHNG-noos. n before c, g, q, x is ng.
  • x is ks. z (Greek words) is dz or zz.
  • h is a light breath, often lost between vowels.
  • Final -m after a vowel nasalises it, as in French: caelum = KAI-loom, IPA [ˈkae̯.ɫũː]. n before s or f does the same, lengthening the vowel.
  • Double consonants are held: an-nus, ec-ce.
  • l is “dark” (as in full) except before i and in ll; r is trilled.

Allen & Greenough cover the sounds in §8 and the alphabet in §1.

Ecclesiastical

The Italianate pronunciation of the Roman Catholic Church: Latin read with the sounds of Italian. Some such Italian-flavoured Latin is old; this standardised form became the Church’s official pronunciation in the early twentieth century, and is what most choirs sing.

Vowels have no length contrast: a (ah), e (eh), i (ee), o (o), u (oo). In the stressed syllable e and o are open, as in Italian. ae and oe are plain e: caelum = CHEH-loom, Phoebus = FEH-boos. y is i. au and eu stay diphthongs.

Consonants — what changes from Classical:

  • c before e, i, y, ae, oe is ch: Cicerō = CHEE-cheh-ro. cc there is t-ch: ecce = EHT-cheh. Elsewhere c is k.
  • g before e, i, y, ae, oe is j: rēgīna = reh-JEE-nah. Elsewhere hard.
  • sc before those vowels is sh: scelus = SHEH-loos; xc is ksh: excelsus = ehk-SHEHL-soos.
  • gn is ny (Italian gnocchi): magnus = MAHN-nyoos.
  • ti before a vowel is tsee: grātia = GRAHT-tsee-ah — except after s, t, x (hostia).
  • v is English v; ph is f; th is t; ch is k.
  • h is silent — except in mihi and nihil, traditionally MEE-kee, NEE-keel.
  • ex- before a vowel, when the accent follows, is egz: exārdeō = ehg-ZAHR-deh-o.
  • z is dz. Final -m is a plain m.

Syllables and accent

Both systems divide words and place the accent the same way, by the classical rules (A&G §7, §12).

A single consonant between vowels begins the next syllable (a-mō); of two or more, the last begins it (hor-tus, mag-nus) — except that a stop followed by r or l usually goes together (pa-tris). x counts as two consonants.

A syllable is heavy if its vowel is long or a diphthong, or if it ends in a consonant. Then:

  • Words of two syllables are stressed on the first: HOR-toos.
  • Longer words are stressed on the second-to-last syllable if it is heavy (a-MĪ-cus, ae-TER-nus), and otherwise on the one before it (DO-mi-nus, TE-ne-brae).

This is why the macrons matter even in Ecclesiastical, which no longer pronounces vowel length: rēgīna and dominus are both three syllables, but the long ī pulls the accent forward in the first.

Why Marvell opens in Ecclesiastical

No one in the seventeenth century spoke “Ecclesiastical Latin” in its modern, standardised form, and Marvell left no account of how he pronounced Latin. The default is an informed guess, and the switch is there to overrule it.

The case for it runs through Milton. In Of Education (1644) Milton told schoolmasters that their pupils’

speech is to be fashion’d to a distinct and clear pronuntiation, as near as may be to the Italian, especially in the Vowels. For we Englishmen being far Northerly, do not open our mouths in the cold air, wide enough to grace a Southern Tongue; but are observ’d by all other Nations to speak exceeding close and inward: So that to smatter Latine with an English mouth, is as ill a hearing as Law-French.

English schools then taught a native pronunciation of Latin that had followed English through its vowel shifts — the “English mouth” Milton objected to. Milton had lived in Italy in 1638–39 and wanted Latin spoken the Italian way. Marvell was his close associate: Milton recommended him for government service in 1653, praising his four years abroad “in Holland, France, Italy, and Spaine”, and in 1657 Marvell became Milton’s assistant in the Latin Secretaryship. Whether Marvell’s Latin followed Milton’s advice is unrecorded; it is plausible, and the Italianate system is the nearest modern stand-in for “as near as may be to the Italian”.

Two cautions. Milton asks for Italian vowels; he says nothing about c and g, and the consonants of seventeenth-century Latin varied from country to country (in Venice, soft c was ts). And Marvell, schooled at Hull and Cambridge, certainly heard the English pronunciation all his youth. Read the Ecclesiastical popovers on Hortus as one plausible voice, not a recording.

How the popovers work it out

The pronunciation is computed, not looked up. The texts are printed without macrons, so the vowel lengths of each form come from its Wiktionary inflection table (mortale in the text is mortāle: mor-TAH-leh); the reader then applies the rules on this page. Checked against Wiktionary’s own transcriptions of every dictionary form in the lexicon, it agrees on about 99% of words in both systems. The rest are mostly compounds whose prefix changes the syllables (ad-stringō), and Greek names.

Where the text leaves a form’s case open and the cases differ in length — vestra (nominative) or vestrā (ablative) — the Classical line shows both; the metre of the line usually decides. Where a form’s lengths aren’t known, the popover pronounces the dictionary form instead and names it.
