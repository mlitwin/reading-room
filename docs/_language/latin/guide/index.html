Pronunciation

Every Latin word popover shows how the word you tapped is pronounced — that form, under the reading its tab shows — as an English-letter respelling with the stressed syllable in capitals, then the IPA. A switch on the popover flips between the two systems described here, and your choice is remembered for each text. Ovid opens in Classical; Marvell’s Hortus opens in Ecclesiastical (see below).

The two systems share their syllables and their accent. They differ in the sounds of a handful of letters — mostly c, g, v, ae, and h.

Classical

The pronunciation of educated Romans of the late Republic and early Empire, reconstructed from ancient grammarians, spelling, loanwords, and verse. It is the system taught in most schools and universities today, and the one Ovid would have recognised.

Vowels. Each vowel is short or long, and length is part of the word: malum “evil” is not mālum “apple”. The popovers take length from the macrons of the dictionary form; an unmarked vowel is short.

Diphthongs. ae as in aisle (ai); au as in house (ow); oe as in boil (oy); eu is e gliding to u; ui in cui, huic is one syllable.

Consonants mostly as in English, with these fixed values:

  • c is always k: Cicerō = KIH-keh-roh. g is always hard: genus = GEH-noos.
  • v (consonantal u) is English w: virgō = WIHR-goh.
  • i before a vowel at the start of a word, and between vowels, is English y: iam, iuvenis. Between vowels it is doubled: maior = MAI-yor.
  • qu is kw; gu after n is gw: lingua = LIHNG-gwah.
  • s is always the s of sing, never z. t is always t: grātia = GRAH-tee-ah.
  • ph, th, ch are p, t, k with a puff of breath, not f, th, ch.
  • gn is ngn: magnus = MAHNG-noos. n before c, g, q, x is ng.
  • x is ks. z (Greek words) is dz or zz.
  • h is a light breath, often lost between vowels.
  • Final -m after a vowel nasalises it, as in French: caelum = KAI-loom, IPA [ˈkae̯.ɫũː]. n before s or f does the same, lengthening the vowel.
  • Double consonants are held: an-nus, ec-ce.
  • l is “dark” (as in full) except before i and in ll; r is trilled.

Allen & Greenough cover the sounds in §8 and the alphabet in §1.

Ecclesiastical

The Italianate pronunciation of the Roman Catholic Church: Latin read with the sounds of Italian. Some such Italian-flavoured Latin is old; this standardised form became the Church’s official pronunciation in the early twentieth century, and is what most choirs sing.

Vowels have no length contrast: a (ah), e (eh), i (ee), o (o), u (oo). In the stressed syllable e and o are open, as in Italian. ae and oe are plain e: caelum = CHEH-loom, Phoebus = FEH-boos. y is i. au and eu stay diphthongs.

Consonants — what changes from Classical:

  • c before e, i, y, ae, oe is ch: Cicerō = CHEE-cheh-ro. cc there is t-ch: ecce = EHT-cheh. Elsewhere c is k.
  • g before e, i, y, ae, oe is j: rēgīna = reh-JEE-nah. Elsewhere hard.
  • sc before those vowels is sh: scelus = SHEH-loos; xc is ksh: excelsus = ehk-SHEHL-soos.
  • gn is ny (Italian gnocchi): magnus = MAHN-nyoos.
  • ti before a vowel is tsee: grātia = GRAHT-tsee-ah — except after s, t, x (hostia).
  • v is English v; ph is f; th is t; ch is k.
  • h is silent — except in mihi and nihil, traditionally MEE-kee, NEE-keel.
  • ex- before a vowel, when the accent follows, is egz: exārdeō = ehg-ZAHR-deh-o.
  • z is dz. Final -m is a plain m.

Syllables and accent

Both systems divide words and place the accent the same way, by the classical rules (A&G §7, §12).

A single consonant between vowels begins the next syllable (a-mō); of two or more, the last begins it (hor-tus, mag-nus) — except that a stop followed by r or l usually goes together (pa-tris). x counts as two consonants.

A syllable is heavy if its vowel is long or a diphthong, or if it ends in a consonant. Then:

  • Words of two syllables are stressed on the first: HOR-toos.
  • Longer words are stressed on the second-to-last syllable if it is heavy (a-MĪ-cus, ae-TER-nus), and otherwise on the one before it (DO-mi-nus, TE-ne-brae).

This is why the macrons matter even in Ecclesiastical, which no longer pronounces vowel length: rēgīna and dominus are both three syllables, but the long ī pulls the accent forward in the first.

Why Marvell opens in Ecclesiastical

No one in the seventeenth century spoke “Ecclesiastical Latin” in its modern, standardised form, and Marvell left no account of how he pronounced Latin. The default is an informed guess, and the switch is there to overrule it.

The case for it runs through Milton. In Of Education (1644) Milton told schoolmasters that their pupils’

speech is to be fashion’d to a distinct and clear pronuntiation, as near as may be to the Italian, especially in the Vowels. For we Englishmen being far Northerly, do not open our mouths in the cold air, wide enough to grace a Southern Tongue; but are observ’d by all other Nations to speak exceeding close and inward: So that to smatter Latine with an English mouth, is as ill a hearing as Law-French.

English schools then taught a native pronunciation of Latin that had followed English through its vowel shifts — the “English mouth” Milton objected to. Milton had lived in Italy in 1638–39 and wanted Latin spoken the Italian way. Marvell was his close associate: Milton recommended him for government service in 1653, praising his four years abroad “in Holland, France, Italy, and Spaine”, and in 1657 Marvell became Milton’s assistant in the Latin Secretaryship. Whether Marvell’s Latin followed Milton’s advice is unrecorded; it is plausible, and the Italianate system is the nearest modern stand-in for “as near as may be to the Italian”.

Two cautions. Milton asks for Italian vowels; he says nothing about c and g, and the consonants of seventeenth-century Latin varied from country to country (in Venice, soft c was ts). And Marvell, schooled at Hull and Cambridge, certainly heard the English pronunciation all his youth. Read the Ecclesiastical popovers on Hortus as one plausible voice, not a recording.

How the popovers work it out

The pronunciation is computed, not looked up. The texts are printed without macrons, so the vowel lengths of each form come from its Wiktionary inflection table (mortale in the text is mortāle: mor-TAH-leh); the reader then applies the rules on this page. Checked against Wiktionary’s own transcriptions of every dictionary form in the lexicon, it agrees on about 99% of words in both systems. The rest are mostly compounds whose prefix changes the syllables (ad-stringō), and Greek names.

Where the text leaves a form’s case open and the cases differ in length — vestra (nominative) or vestrā (ablative) — the Classical line shows both; the metre of the line usually decides. Where a form’s lengths aren’t known, the popover pronounces the dictionary form instead and names it.

Part of Speech

Noun n.

A word that names a person, place, thing, or idea. In Latin, nouns inflect for case (six forms — nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative, vocative) and number (singular and plural). Every noun belongs to one of five declensions and carries a fixed gender (masculine, feminine, or neuter), shown in the dictionary entry.

Allen & Greenough: §36

Verb v.

A word that names an action or a state. A Latin verb inflects for person, number, tense, mood, and voice. One verb has roughly 150 distinct forms; the four principal parts listed in the dictionary entry are enough to generate them all.

Allen & Greenough: §153

Adjective adj.

A word that modifies a noun. Latin adjectives agree with the noun they modify in case, number, and gender — so an adjective has all three gender forms and shifts among them. The dictionary entry shows the three nominative-singular endings (e.g. novus, -a, -um).

Allen & Greenough: §109

Pronoun pron.

A word that stands in for a noun (he, she, they, that one). Latin pronouns inflect for case, number, and gender like adjectives, often with irregular forms in some slots.

Allen & Greenough: §140

Preposition prep.

An indeclinable word that introduces a noun phrase and specifies its grammatical relationship to the rest of the sentence (in urbe, “in the city”). Each preposition governs a specific case — usually accusative or ablative; the dictionary entry says which.

Allen & Greenough: §219

Conjunction conj.

An indeclinable word that joins clauses or phrases (et, “and”; sed, “but”; quod, “because”). Latin distinguishes coordinating (et) from subordinating (ut, cum) conjunctions.

Allen & Greenough: §222

Adverb adv.

An indeclinable word that modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb (celeriter, “quickly”; iam, “already”). Latin adverbs do not inflect, though many form comparative and superlative degrees.

Allen & Greenough: §213

Interjection interj.

An invariant exclamatory word, used to express emotion or call attention (o!, “oh!”; ecce, “behold!”). Interjections stand outside the grammatical structure of the sentence.

Allen & Greenough: §225

Enclitic encl.

A particle attached to the end of the preceding word rather than written separately (-que = “and”; -ne = question marker; -ve = “or”). Primaque in dictionary order is prima + -que.

Allen & Greenough: §324

Numeral num.

A word that denotes number — cardinal (unus, duo, tres; “one”, “two”, “three”), ordinal (primus, secundus; “first”, “second”), distributive (singuli, bini; “one each”, “two each”), or multiplicative (semel, bis; “once”, “twice”). Some are indeclinable; unus, duo, tres and the hundreds inflect.

Allen & Greenough: §132

Case

Nominative nom.

The case of the grammatical subject and of predicate nouns/adjectives (puella ridet, “the girl smiles”). It's also the dictionary-entry form of a Latin noun.

Allen & Greenough: §339

Genitive gen.

The case of possession and origin (puellae liber, “the girl's book”), and of dependence on certain nouns and verbs. The genitive singular is the second principal part of a noun's dictionary entry — it shows which declension the noun belongs to.

Allen & Greenough: §341

Dative dat.

The case of the indirect object and the recipient (puellae librum dedit, “he gave a book to the girl”). Also used with certain adjectives (“dear to her”) and verbs (“trust in him”).

Allen & Greenough: §360

Accusative acc.

The case of the direct object (puellam amat, “he loves the girl”), and the case governed by many prepositions of motion-toward (in urbem, “into the city”). For neuter nouns and adjectives, the accusative form always equals the nominative — both singular and plural.

Allen & Greenough: §386

Ablative abl.

A case of separation, means, manner, time, and place (gladio interfecit, “killed with a sword”). Also the case governed by many prepositions of rest-in and motion-from (in urbe, “in the city”; ex urbe, “out of the city”). Has no direct English equivalent.

Allen & Greenough: §398

Vocative voc.

The case of direct address (Brute!, “Brutus!”). For most nouns the vocative is identical to the nominative; second-declension -us nouns have a distinct -e vocative.

Allen & Greenough: §340

Locative loc.

The case of place-where, surviving only for names of towns and small islands and a few common nouns (Romae, “at Rome”; domi, “at home”; ruri, “in the country”; humi, “on the ground”). Elsewhere Latin expresses place-where with in + ablative.

Allen & Greenough: §427

Number

Singular sg.

A grammatical number used when one of something is meant.

Allen & Greenough: §35

Plural pl.

A grammatical number used when more than one of something is meant. Latin has no dual number; one or many is the only distinction.

Allen & Greenough: §35

Gender

Masculine m.

One of the three Latin grammatical genders. Lexical gender — most nouns are assigned a gender that's just a fact about them; it doesn't always track sex (nauta, “sailor”, is masculine but ends like a feminine).

Allen & Greenough: §29

Feminine f.

One of the three Latin grammatical genders. Like masculine, lexical and largely arbitrary.

Allen & Greenough: §29

Neuter n.

One of the three Latin grammatical genders. Neuter nouns and adjectives always have the same form in the nominative and accusative (whether singular or plural); the plural nominative/accusative always ends in -a.

Allen & Greenough: §29

Common gender c.

Nouns that may be either masculine or feminine according to the sex of the being they name (civis, “citizen”; incola, “inhabitant”; caelicola, “heaven-dweller”) are said to be of common gender. The noun takes whichever gender fits its referent in context.

Allen & Greenough: §34

Person

1st person 1.

Refers to the speaker — I in the singular, we in the plural. In Latin the person is shown by the verb ending; pronouns (ego, nos) appear only when emphatic or contrastive.

Allen & Greenough: §163

2nd person 2.

Refers to the addressee — you in the singular, you (plural) in the plural. Latin has distinct singular and plural pronouns (tu, vos); the verb ending also distinguishes them.

Allen & Greenough: §163

3rd person 3.

Refers to a non-speaker, non-addressee — he, she, it, they. There's no Latin pronoun for “it” as such; is, ea, id or demonstrative ille, illa, illud fill the role.

Allen & Greenough: §163

Tense

Present pres.

The tense of action happening now or generally true (amat, “he loves” / “he is loving”). Built on the present stem (the first principal part).

Allen & Greenough: §464

Imperfect impf.

The past tense of ongoing or repeated action (amabat, “he was loving” / “he used to love”). Built on the present stem with -ba- infix.

Allen & Greenough: §464

Future fut.

The tense of action yet to happen (amabit, “he will love”). Different conjugations form the future with different endings.

Allen & Greenough: §464

Perfect perf.

The tense of completed action (amavit, “he loved” / “he has loved”). Built on the perfect stem (the third principal part).

Allen & Greenough: §464

Pluperfect plpf.

The past tense of action completed before another past action (amaverat, “he had loved”). Built on the perfect stem with -era- infix.

Allen & Greenough: §464

Future perfect fut. pf.

The tense of action that will have been completed by some future point (amaverit, “he will have loved”). Built on the perfect stem with -eri- infix.

Allen & Greenough: §464

Mood

Indicative ind.

The mood of statement and question — the default mood for asserting that something is, was, or will be the case (amat, “he loves”).

Allen & Greenough: §437

Subjunctive subj.

The mood of possibility, wish, command in subordinate clauses, hypothetical condition, and other non-assertive uses (amet, “may he love” / “let him love”). One of the most flexible features of Latin.

Allen & Greenough: §438

Imperative imp.

The mood of direct command (ama!, “love!”). Latin has 2nd-person singular and plural imperatives (and rare 3rd-person forms); the singular often equals the present stem with no ending.

Allen & Greenough: §448

Infinitive inf.

A non-finite verb form that names the action itself (“to love”, “to be loved”). Latin has six infinitives — present/perfect/future × active/passive — though the present active is the most common. The present active infinitive is the second principal part of a verb's dictionary entry.

Allen & Greenough: §451

Present active participle pres. act. pcpl.

A non-finite verbal adjective expressing ongoing action by the subject (amans, “loving”). It declines as a third-declension adjective with one ending, agreeing with its noun in case, number, and gender, and is built on the present stem.

Allen & Greenough: §488

Perfect passive participle perf. pass. pcpl.

The verbal adjective formed from the perfect stem of a verb's passive voice (“having been loved”). It declines like a first/second declension adjective (so it agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender) and appears throughout the passive system, in deponent verbs, and in ablative absolute constructions. The fourth principal part of a verb gives this stem (in the neuter form, amatum).

Allen & Greenough: §488

Future active participle fut. act. pcpl.

A non-finite verbal adjective expressing intended or impending action by the subject (amaturus, “about to love”). It declines like a first/second declension adjective and combines with forms of esse to make the active periphrastic conjugation.

Allen & Greenough: §488

Gerundive gerundive

A non-finite verbal adjective expressing necessity or impending action upon the subject — traditionally also called the future passive participle (amandus, “to be loved” / “requiring to be loved”). It declines like a first/second declension adjective, agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender, and forms the passive periphrastic with esse. When a gerund would take an accusative object, Latin prefers the gerundive construction.

Allen & Greenough: §500, §503

Gerund ger.

A verbal noun expressing the action of the verb (amandi, “of loving”). It is the neuter singular of the gerundive used substantively, and exists only in the oblique cases — genitive, dative, accusative (after prepositions), and ablative; the nominative slot is filled by the infinitive. As a noun it is itself governed by other words; as a verb it may take an object in the proper case.

Allen & Greenough: §501, §502

Voice

Active act.

The voice in which the subject is the agent of the action (puer librum legit, “the boy reads the book”).

Allen & Greenough: §156

Passive pass.

The voice in which the subject undergoes the action (liber a puero legitur, “the book is read by the boy”). Latin has distinct passive endings for the present system; the perfect passive is built from the perfect passive participle + a form of esse.

Allen & Greenough: §156

Degree

Comparative comp.

The degree of an adjective or adverb expressing “more” or “rather/too” (altior, “higher”; altius, “more deeply”). Adjective comparatives are formed with -ior (neuter -ius) and decline as third-declension adjectives; the adverb's comparative is the neuter accusative of the adjective's. The compared term takes quam or the ablative of comparison.

Allen & Greenough: §123, §124

Superlative superl.

The degree of an adjective or adverb expressing “most” or “very” (altissimus, “highest / very high”; carissime, “dearest”, vocative). Formed with -issimus (-rimus after -er, -limus for a few in -lis) and declined like a first/second declension adjective. The positive degree is unmarked in parse codes.

Allen & Greenough: §123, §124

Grammar Terms

Supine

A defective verbal noun in -um (or -u), used in a narrow set of constructions: with verbs of motion to express purpose (venit spectatum, “he came to see”), and as the fourth principal part of a verb’s dictionary entry — the form that gives the stem for the perfect passive participle and the future active participle.

Allen & Greenough: §508

Gender

The three Latin grammatical genders are masculine, feminine, and neuter. Nouns have a fixed gender; adjectives shift to match the noun they modify.

Allen & Greenough: §29

Declension

One of five patterns of noun (and adjective) inflection in Latin, identified by the genitive singular ending. 1st declension: -ae (feminine, like forma). 2nd: -i (masculine and neuter). 3rd: -is (all three genders). 4th: -us (mostly masculine). 5th: -ei (mostly feminine).

Allen & Greenough: §36

Principal parts

The set of four verb forms a dictionary entry gives so the reader can build any inflected form. For a regular verb: 1st = the lemma’s dictionary form (1st-person singular present indicative active, e.g. amo); 2nd = present active infinitive (amare); 3rd = 1st-person singular perfect indicative active (amavi); 4th = supine (amatum).

Allen & Greenough: §164

First principal part

The first slot in a verb’s dictionary entry — the 1st-person singular present indicative active (e.g. amo, “I love”). This is the form the lemma is filed under, and it gives the present stem from which all present-system forms are built.

Allen & Greenough: §164

Second principal part

The second slot in a verb’s dictionary entry — the present active infinitive (e.g. amare, “to love”). It shows which conjugation the verb belongs to and how the present system inflects.

Allen & Greenough: §164

Third principal part

The third slot in a verb’s dictionary entry — the 1st-person singular perfect indicative active (e.g. amavi, “I have loved”). It gives the perfect stem, from which all forms in the perfect, pluperfect, and future-perfect active are built.

Allen & Greenough: §164

Fourth principal part

The fourth slot in a verb’s dictionary entry — the supine (e.g. amatum). It gives the stem for the perfect passive participle and the future active participle.

Allen & Greenough: §164
