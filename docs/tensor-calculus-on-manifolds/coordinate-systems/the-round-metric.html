The round metric

Matthew Litwin 2026-06-23

The round metric on S2S^2S2, induced from S2⊆R3S^2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3S2⊆R3, in the three charts of this chapter.

In spherical coordinates

Pull back the Euclidean metric dX2+dY2+dZ2dX^2 + dY^2 + dZ^2dX2+dY2+dZ2 on R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 through the parametrization Φ(θ,φ)\Phi(\theta, \varphi)Φ(θ,φ):

g=dθ2+sin⁡2θ  dφ2.\tens{g} = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\; d\varphi^2. g=dθ2+sin2θdφ2.

The component matrix is

[gμν]=(100sin⁡2θ),det⁡g=sin⁡2θ.[g_{\mu\nu}] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sin^2\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det g = \sin^2\theta. [gμν​]=(10​0sin2θ​),detg=sin2θ.

Riemannian (both eigenvalues positive), non-degenerate where sin⁡θ≠0\sin\theta \neq 0sinθ=0 — i.e. everywhere the chart covers. At the poles sin⁡θ=0\sin\theta = 0sinθ=0 and the chart breaks down; the metric is fine there but (θ,φ)(\theta, \varphi)(θ,φ) are bad coordinates.

The inverse metric is

[gμν]=(1001/sin⁡2θ).[g^{\mu\nu}] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/\sin^2\theta \end{pmatrix}. [gμν]=(10​01/sin2θ​).

The orthogonality g(∂θ,∂φ)=0\tens{g}(\partial_\theta, \partial_\varphi) = 0g(∂θ​,∂φ​)=0 says the spherical coordinates are an orthogonal coordinate system on S2S^2S2.

Raising and lowering

The in action: the covector dual to ∂θ\partial_\theta∂θ​ is

(∂θ)♭=gθν dxν=dθ.(\partial_\theta)^\flat = g_{\theta\nu}\, dx^\nu = d\theta. (∂θ​)♭=gθν​dxν=dθ.

The takeaways:

  • The same geometric metric has different component matrices in different charts.
  • Off-diagonal entries are a chart artifact, not a feature of the geometry.
  • det⁡g\det gdetg is unchanged here only because the chart change has Jacobian determinant 111; in general it scales by J−2J^{-2}J−2.
  • The K=1K = 1K=1 (chapter 8) is the same in both charts because it’s an intrinsic invariant.

In stereographic coordinates

Pulling back the same Euclidean metric through the stereographic chart gives

g=4(1+x2+y2)2 (dx2+dy2).\tens{g} = \frac{4}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^2}\, (dx^2 + dy^2). g=(1+x2+y2)24​(dx2+dy2).

Conformally flat: the metric is a positive scalar function times dx2+dy2dx^2 + dy^2dx2+dy2, so angles agree with Euclidean angles in this chart even though lengths don’t. The conformal factor 4/(1+r2)24/(1 + r^2)^24/(1+r2)2 tends to 000 as r→∞r \to \inftyr→∞, where the south pole sits at infinity.

Every Riemannian 222-manifold admits isothermal (conformally flat) coordinates locally. The sphere happens to admit them on a chart missing a single point.

Musical isomorphism

The metric’s canonical identification of vectors with covectors. On a bare manifold the tangent space and its have the same dimension but no canonical isomorphism between them (contrast the double dual, where V∗∗≅VV^{**} \cong VV∗∗≅V is canonical). A metric g\tens{g}g provides one — the musical isomorphisms

♭:TpM→Tp∗M,v♭:=g(v,⋅),♯:=♭−1:Tp∗M→TpM,\flat: T_p M \to T^*_p M, \qquad \tens{v}^\flat := \tens{g}(\tens{v}, \cdot), \qquad \sharp := \flat^{-1}: T^*_p M \to T_p M, ♭:Tp​M→Tp∗​M,v♭:=g(v,⋅),♯:=♭−1:Tp∗​M→Tp​M,

named for how they move the component index down (vμ=gμν vνv_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}\, v^\nuvμ​=gμν​vν) and up (ωμ=gμν ων\omega^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\, \omega_\nuωμ=gμνων​) — raising and lowering applied to a single index.

The gradient is the standard illustration of the distinction ♯\sharp♯ erases: the differential dfdfdf of a function is metric-free, but the gradient grad⁡f:=(df)♯\operatorname{grad} f := (df)^\sharpgradf:=(df)♯ is not. In Cartesian coordinates on Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn the two have identical components, which is why elementary vector calculus never distinguishes them; in any chart with gμν≠δμνg_{\mu\nu} \neq \delta_{\mu\nu}gμν​=δμν​ they differ.
