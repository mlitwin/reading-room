Newton–Cartan gravity

Matthew Litwin 2026-06-23

The Newtonian limit recovers Newton’s gravity from general relativity approximately. Cartan (1923) showed that Newton’s theory can be stated exactly in the same geometric language. Gravity becomes the curvature of a spacetime connection, free fall becomes geodesic motion, and Poisson’s equation becomes a curvature equation. What Newtonian spacetime lacks is a spacetime metric. Units are G=1G = 1G=1.

Galilean structure

Newtonian spacetime M≅R4M \cong \mathbb{R}^4M≅R4 carries two degenerate structures in place of a Lorentzian metric:

  • an absolute time 1-form τ=dt\tens{\tau} = dtτ=dt, closed, whose level sets are the instants of simultaneity;
  • a spatial metric hμνh^{\mu\nu}hμν, a symmetric (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0)-tensor of rank 3 that measures lengths within each instant, with hμντν=0h^{\mu\nu}\tau_\nu = 0hμντν​=0.

In Galilean coordinates (t,xi)(t, x^i)(t,xi), τμ=(1,0,0,0)\tau_\mu = (1, 0, 0, 0)τμ​=(1,0,0,0) and hμν=diag(0,1,1,1)h^{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(0, 1, 1, 1)hμν=diag(0,1,1,1). Neither tensor is invertible. There are no musical isomorphisms here, and there is no Levi-Civita theorem: compatibility with τ\tens{\tau}τ and hhh does not determine a connection.

What it clarifies

  • Gravity is curvature even without relativity. The geometric picture, in which free fall is geodesic motion and tides are curvature, does not depend on the speed of light. What relativity changes is the structure the connection must respect: a Lorentzian metric instead of a Galilean pair.
  • The potential is frame-dependent; the connection is not. Passing to Galilean coordinates that accelerate with a(t)\tens{a}(t)a(t) shifts Φ→Φ+a(t)⋅x\Phi \to \Phi + \tens{a}(t) \cdot \tens{x}Φ→Φ+a(t)⋅x but describes the same connection. Only ∂i∂jΦ\partial_i\partial_j\Phi∂i​∂j​Φ, the curvature, is frame-independent. A uniform gravitational field can be transformed away, as the equivalence principle says.
  • Two geometrizations of Φ\PhiΦ. The Jacobi metric absorbs the potential into a spatial metric at fixed energy. Newton–Cartan absorbs it into a spacetime connection for all energies at once. Only the second extends to general relativity.

Lovelock's theorem

In four dimensions, every symmetric (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2)-tensor that is (i) built from the metric and at most its first and second derivatives and (ii) divergence-free is a linear combination

a Gμν+b gμνa\, G_{\mu\nu} + b\, g_{\mu\nu} aGμν​+bgμν​

of the Einstein tensor and the metric. So the left-hand side of the Einstein equations — including the cosmological-constant term — is forced, not chosen, once a tensorial second-order theory of the metric is demanded. In dimension n>4n > 4n>4, additional “Lovelock terms” (Gauss–Bonnet and higher) become available, which is one reason higher-dimensional gravity theories are less rigid than $4$D GR.
