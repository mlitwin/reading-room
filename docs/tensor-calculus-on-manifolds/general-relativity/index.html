General relativity

Matthew Litwin 2026-06-23

General relativity is the geometric theory of gravity. It is built on the geometry of Part III, with the mechanics of Parts II and IV and the matter description of chapter 14 as inputs. Spacetime is a Lorentzian 444-manifold; matter and energy are described by a stress–energy tensor TμνT_{\mu\nu}Tμν​; the metric is determined by the , which equate the Einstein tensor (geometry) to a constant times TμνT_{\mu\nu}Tμν​ (matter content).

Five pages:

  1. The Einstein equations and their geometric content.
  2. Schwarzschild: the torsion-free worked example, with Kepler alongside.
  3. Einstein–Cartan: gravity with torsion, coupled to spin.
  4. The Newtonian limit: g00≈−(1+2Φ)g_{00} \approx -(1 + 2\Phi)g00​≈−(1+2Φ), Poisson’s equation, and tides.
  5. Newton–Cartan gravity: Newton’s theory as exact spacetime geometry, with gravity as curvature of a connection and no metric.

This is tensor calculus for GR, not a survey of GR: the field equations, one worked solution, a torsionful variant, and the Newtonian limit. Standard textbooks pick up from here.

Lovelock's theorem

In four dimensions, every symmetric (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2)-tensor that is (i) built from the metric and at most its first and second derivatives and (ii) divergence-free is a linear combination

a Gμν+b gμνa\, G_{\mu\nu} + b\, g_{\mu\nu} aGμν​+bgμν​

of the Einstein tensor and the metric. So the left-hand side of the Einstein equations — including the cosmological-constant term — is forced, not chosen, once a tensorial second-order theory of the metric is demanded. In dimension n>4n > 4n>4, additional “Lovelock terms” (Gauss–Bonnet and higher) become available, which is one reason higher-dimensional gravity theories are less rigid than $4$D GR.
