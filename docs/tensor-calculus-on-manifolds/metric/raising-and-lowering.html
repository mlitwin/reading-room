Raising and lowering, trace, volume form

Matthew Litwin 2026-06-23

The metric and its inverse provide a canonical isomorphism between TpMT_p MTp​M and Tp∗MT^*_p MTp∗​M. This is the operation called raising and lowering indices — it lets the position of an index on a tensor be moved freely, with the rule that lowering uses gμνg_{\mu\nu}gμν​ and raising uses gμνg^{\mu\nu}gμν.

The musical isomorphisms

Define ♭:TpM→Tp∗M\flat: T_p M \to T^*_p M♭:Tp​M→Tp∗​M (flat, lowering) and ♯:Tp∗M→TpM\sharp: T^*_p M \to T_p M♯:Tp∗​M→Tp​M (sharp, raising) by

v♭(w):=g(v,w),g(ω♯,w):=ω(w).\tens{v}^\flat(\tens{w}) := \tens{g}(\tens{v}, \tens{w}), \qquad \tens{g}(\tens{\omega}^\sharp, \tens{w}) := \tens{\omega}(\tens{w}). v♭(w):=g(v,w),g(ω♯,w):=ω(w).

In components,

vμ=gμν vν,ωμ=gμν ων.v_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}\, v^\nu, \qquad \omega^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\, \omega_\nu. vμ​=gμν​vν,ωμ=gμνων​.

The two maps are mutual inverses: (v♭)♯=v(\tens{v}^\flat)^\sharp = \tens{v}(v♭)♯=v and (ω♯)♭=ω(\tens{\omega}^\sharp)^\flat = \tens{\omega}(ω♯)♭=ω.

The standard example of what ♯\sharp♯ buys: the differential dfdfdf of a function is defined on any smooth manifold, but the gradient grad⁡f:=(df)♯\operatorname{grad} f := (df)^\sharpgradf:=(df)♯ requires the metric. In Cartesian coordinates on Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn the two have identical components — which is why vector calculus never needs the distinction; in any chart with gμν≠δμνg_{\mu\nu} \neq \delta_{\mu\nu}gμν​=δμν​ (polar coordinates already) they differ.

For tensors of higher rank, raising and lowering act on a chosen index. Convention: write the same kernel letter and move the index;

Tμν:=gμρ Tρν,Tμν=gμρ Tρν.T_\mu{}^\nu := g_{\mu\rho}\, T^{\rho\nu}, \qquad T^{\mu\nu} = g^{\mu\rho}\, T_\rho{}^\nu. Tμ​ν:=gμρ​Tρν,Tμν=gμρTρ​ν.

The horizontal position is kept (so you know which slot was originally where). Different conventions exist; the slot order is the safe disambiguator.

Trace of a (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1)-tensor

For a (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1)-tensor TμνT^\mu{}_\nuTμν​ — equivalently a linear endomorphism of TpMT_p MTp​M — the trace is the

tr T:=Tμμ.\mathrm{tr}\, \tens{T} := T^\mu{}_\mu. trT:=Tμμ​.

No metric needed. For a (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2)-tensor TμνT_{\mu\nu}Tμν​, the trace requires raising one index:

trgT:=gμν Tμν=Tμμ.\mathrm{tr}_g \tens{T} := g^{\mu\nu}\, T_{\mu\nu} = T^\mu{}_\mu. trg​T:=gμνTμν​=Tμμ​.

Orientation

An orientation of an nnn-manifold is a nowhere-vanishing nnn-form, taken up to multiplication by a positive function. A chart is positively oriented when dx1∧⋯∧dxndx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^ndx1∧⋯∧dxn agrees with it. An orientation is what makes ∫Mω\int_M \tens{\omega}∫M​ω of an nnn-form well-defined: the change-of-variables Jacobian then always has positive sign. The Möbius strip and Klein bottle admit none. Lovelock's theorem

In four dimensions, every symmetric (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2)-tensor that is (i) built from the metric and at most its first and second derivatives and (ii) divergence-free is a linear combination

a Gμν+b gμνa\, G_{\mu\nu} + b\, g_{\mu\nu} aGμν​+bgμν​

of the Einstein tensor and the metric. So the left-hand side of the Einstein equations — including the cosmological-constant term — is forced, not chosen, once a tensorial second-order theory of the metric is demanded. In dimension n>4n > 4n>4, additional “Lovelock terms” (Gauss–Bonnet and higher) become available, which is one reason higher-dimensional gravity theories are less rigid than $4$D GR.
