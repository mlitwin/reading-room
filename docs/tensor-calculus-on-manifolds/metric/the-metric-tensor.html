Signature

The symmetric bilinear form gpg_pgp​ at a point is classified up to choice of basis by its signature (p,q)(p, q)(p,q) with p+q=np + q = np+q=n — the number of positive and negative eigenvalues. (The pointwise linear algebra — Sylvester’s law, the lightcone at a single event — is previewed in the Part I aside.) Three cases come up:

  • Riemannian: signature (n,0)(n, 0)(n,0), i.e. ggg is positive-definite. g(v,v)>0g(v, v) > 0g(v,v)>0 for v≠0v \neq 0v=0. Every vvv has a positive length g(v,v)\sqrt{g(v, v)}g(v,v)​.
  • Lorentzian: signature (1,n−1)(1, n-1)(1,n−1) or (n−1,1)(n-1, 1)(n−1,1) depending on convention; one direction is “timelike” and the rest are “spacelike.” General relativity uses Lorentzian signature on a 444-manifold with convention (−,+,+,+)(-, +, +, +)(−,+,+,+) (so timelike vectors have g(v,v)<0g(v, v) < 0g(v,v)<0) or (+,−,−,−)(+, -, -, -)(+,−,−,−) (the opposite).
  • Pseudo-Riemannian: any non-degenerate signature, generalizing both above.

The signature is a discrete invariant — it cannot change continuously over a connected manifold. A Lorentzian metric distinguishes three classes of tangent vector at each point:

  • Timelike if g(v,v)<0g(v, v) < 0g(v,v)<0 (in mostly-plus convention) — into the future or past lightcone.
  • Null (or lightlike) if g(v,v)=0g(v, v) = 0g(v,v)=0 — on the lightcone.
  • Spacelike if g(v,v)>0g(v, v) > 0g(v,v)>0 — outside the lightcone.

A Lorentzian manifold also needs a time orientation — a continuous choice of “future” lightcone at each point — to do physics. Not every Lorentzian manifold admits one; those that do are called time-orientable.

Existence

Any paracompact manifold — which here means every manifold in this book, since Hausdorff implies paracompact — admits a Riemannian metric, by a construction. Lorentzian metrics are much more restrictive: a closed orientable nnn-manifold admits a Lorentzian metric iff it has a nowhere-vanishing vector field, equivalently iff its Euler characteristic vanishes. Among compact 222-manifolds, only the torus and Klein bottle admit Lorentzian metrics; the sphere does not. (S2S^2S2 has Euler characteristic 222.)

This is one reason GR is set on non-compact spacetimes (open 444-manifolds, asymptotically flat or otherwise) rather than compact ones: a closed 444-manifold needs vanishing Euler characteristic just to carry a Lorentzian metric at all.

Second countable

A topological space is second countable if its topology has a countable base: countably many open sets such that every open set is a union of some of them. Equivalently for metric spaces, XXX has a countable dense subset and balls of rational radius centered on that subset form a base. This is what makes paracompactness and partition-of-unity arguments work on a manifold.

Embedded manifold

A smooth submanifold of RN\mathbb{R}^NRN is a subset M⊆RNM \subseteq \mathbb{R}^NM⊆RN that locally looks like the graph of a smooth function: around every p∈Mp \in Mp∈M there is an open U⊆RNU \subseteq \mathbb{R}^NU⊆RN, an open V⊆RnV \subseteq \mathbb{R}^nV⊆Rn, and a smooth embedding Φ:V→U∩M\Phi: V \to U \cap MΦ:V→U∩M whose differential is everywhere injective. The image Φ(V)\Phi(V)Φ(V) is an open piece of MMM, and the nnn coordinates on VVV are a chart on MMM.

The embedded view of differential geometry takes advantage of the ambient RN\mathbb{R}^NRN: tangent vectors are vectors in RN\mathbb{R}^NRN (sitting in the affine tangent plane to MMM); the metric is the pullback of the Euclidean inner product; integration is over an embedded submanifold of RN\mathbb{R}^NRN. The Whitney embedding theorem says every smooth nnn-manifold can be embedded in R2n\mathbb{R}^{2n}R2n, so the embedded view loses no generality in principle. In practice it can be inconvenient — Lorentzian spacetimes do not embed isometrically in any flat ambient — which is why the abstract view is also needed.

Hausdorff

A topological space is Hausdorff if any two distinct points p,qp, qp,q have disjoint open neighborhoods U∋pU \ni pU∋p, V∋qV \ni qV∋q with U∩V=∅U \cap V = \emptysetU∩V=∅. Most spaces appearing in differential geometry are Hausdorff: Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn, every metric space, every manifold, every Lie group. The non-Hausdorff cases are exotic — the “line with two origins” is the canonical example.
