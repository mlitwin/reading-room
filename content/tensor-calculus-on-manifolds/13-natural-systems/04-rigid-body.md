---
title: The rigid body
---

A rigid body is the standard natural system whose kinetic metric is not flat. Its configuration space is a Lie group, and the motion is geodesic for a metric built from the inertia tensor. Notation on this page follows Marsden–Ratiu: $\tens{\omega}$ and $\tens{\Omega}$ are angular velocities, not forms.

## Configuration space

Label the body's points by their positions $\tens{X}$ in a reference configuration, with the center of mass at the origin. A configuration is a rotation $R \in SO(3)$ and a translation $\tens{c} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, placing the point $\tens{X}$ at
$$\tens{x} = R\tens{X} + \tens{c}, \qquad Q = \mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3).$$
Differentiating, $\dot{\tens{x}} = \dot R\, \tens{X} + \dot{\tens{c}}$. The matrices $\dot R R^{-1}$ and $R^{-1}\dot R$ are antisymmetric, so each corresponds to a vector via $\hat{\tens{a}}\, \tens{b} := \tens{a} \times \tens{b}$:
$$\dot R R^{-1} = \hat{\tens{\omega}}, \qquad R^{-1}\dot R = \hat{\tens{\Omega}}, \qquad \tens{\omega} = R\tens{\Omega}.$$
$\tens{\omega}$ is the **spatial angular velocity**, seen from the laboratory. $\tens{\Omega}$ is the **body angular velocity**, the same vector expressed in the body's own axes.

## The kinetic metric

With mass density $\rho(\tens{X})$ and total mass $M$, the kinetic energy splits into translation and rotation. The cross term vanishes because the center of mass is at the origin:
$$T = \tfrac12 M\, \lvert \dot{\tens{c}} \rvert^2 + \tfrac12\, \tens{\Omega} \cdot \mathbb{I}\, \tens{\Omega}, \qquad \mathbb{I}_{ab} = \int \rho\, \bigl(\lvert \tens{X} \rvert^2 \delta_{ab} - X_a X_b\bigr)\, d^3X.$$
The **inertia tensor** $\mathbb{I}$ is a constant symmetric positive-definite matrix in body coordinates. Its eigenvalues $I_1, I_2, I_3$ are the principal moments.

The rotational part is a Riemannian metric on $SO(3)$. Rotating the laboratory replaces $R$ by $R_0 R$ for a fixed $R_0$, which leaves $\tens{\Omega} = R^{-1}\dot R$ unchanged. So the metric is **left-invariant**: it is determined by its value $\mathbb{I}$ at the identity and carried around the group by left multiplication. Unless $\mathbb{I} \propto 1$, it is not also right-invariant: rotating the body before placing it changes $T$. With the translation decoupled, free rotation is geodesic motion on $SO(3)$ with this metric. The metric is curved for every $\mathbb{I}$; for $\mathbb{I} \propto 1$ it is the round metric of $\mathbb{RP}^3$.

## Euler's equations

The symmetry group is left multiplication, that is, rotations of space, generated by the three right-invariant vector fields on $SO(3)$. By [Noether's theorem](../08-symmetry-and-noether/01-noethers-theorem.md), its conserved charge is the **spatial angular momentum**
$$\tens{\pi} = R\, \tens{\Pi}, \qquad \tens{\Pi} := \mathbb{I}\, \tens{\Omega} \quad \text{(body angular momentum)}.$$
Differentiating $\tens{\pi} = \text{const}$ gives $\dot R\, \tens{\Pi} + R\, \dot{\tens{\Pi}} = 0$. Multiply by $R^{-1}$ and use $R^{-1}\dot R = \hat{\tens{\Omega}}$:
$$\boxed{\quad \dot{\tens{\Pi}} = \tens{\Pi} \times \tens{\Omega} \quad} \qquad \text{i.e.} \quad I_1 \dot\Omega_1 = (I_2 - I_3)\, \Omega_2 \Omega_3 \text{ and cyclic.}$$
These are **Euler's equations**. They are first-order equations in the body frame, solved before reconstructing $R(t)$ from $\dot R = R\hat{\tens{\Omega}}$. Two quantities are conserved: the energy $T = \tfrac12\, \tens{\Pi} \cdot \tens{\Omega}$ and $\lvert \tens{\Pi} \rvert^2 = \lvert \tens{\pi} \rvert^2$. Each solution moves along an intersection of an energy ellipsoid with an angular-momentum sphere. Rotation about the largest or smallest principal axis is stable; rotation about the middle axis is unstable (the tennis-racket theorem).

Euler's equations are the prototype of reduction by symmetry. Geodesic flow on a Lie group with a left-invariant metric, written in body variables, gives equations on the Lie algebra alone (the Euler–Arnold equations). The same structure describes ideal fluid flow, with $SO(3)$ replaced by the group of volume-preserving diffeomorphisms.

## The heavy top

Fix one point of the body (not the center of mass) at the origin. The configuration space is $SO(3)$, and $\mathbb{I}$ is taken about the fixed point. Let gravity act along $-Z$. The potential $V = M g\, \hat{\tens{Z}} \cdot R\, \tens{\chi}$, with $\tens{\chi}$ the body-frame position of the center of mass, is invariant only under rotations about the vertical. As for the [spherical pendulum](03-symmetry-and-the-sphere.md), one component of angular momentum survives, $\pi_Z$, along with the energy. The symmetric (Lagrange) top has one more conserved quantity, $\Pi_3$, and is integrable.
