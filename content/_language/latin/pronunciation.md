Every Latin word popover shows how the word you tapped is pronounced — that
form, under the reading its tab shows — as an English-letter respelling with the
stressed syllable in capitals, then the IPA.
A switch on the popover flips between the two systems described here, and your
choice is remembered for each text. Ovid opens in **Classical**; Marvell's
*Hortus* opens in **Ecclesiastical** (see [below](#pron-why-marvell-opens-in-ecclesiastical)).

The two systems share their syllables and their accent. They differ in the
sounds of a handful of letters — mostly *c*, *g*, *v*, *ae*, and *h*.

## Classical

The pronunciation of educated Romans of the late Republic and early Empire,
reconstructed from ancient grammarians, spelling, loanwords, and verse. It is
the system taught in most schools and universities today, and the one Ovid
would have recognised.

**Vowels.** Each vowel is short or long, and length is part of the word: *malum*
"evil" is not *mālum* "apple". The popovers take length from the macrons of the
dictionary form; an unmarked vowel is short.

| Letter | Sound | Respelled |
|---|---|---|
| a, ā | *a* as in *father* (short ā is only shorter) | ah |
| e | *e* as in *pet* | eh |
| ē | *ey* as in *they*, without the glide | ay |
| i | *i* as in *pit* | ih |
| ī | *i* as in *machine* | ee |
| o | *o* as in British *pot* | o |
| ō | *o* as in *note*, without the glide | oh |
| u, ū | *u* as in *put*; *oo* as in *rule* | oo |
| y | French *u*, German *ü* (Greek words) | ü |

**Diphthongs.** *ae* as in *aisle* (ai); *au* as in *house* (ow); *oe* as in
*boil* (oy); *eu* is *e* gliding to *u*; *ui* in *cui*, *huic* is one syllable.

**Consonants** mostly as in English, with these fixed values:

- **c** is always *k*: *Cicerō* = KIH-keh-roh. **g** is always hard: *genus* = GEH-noos.
- **v** (consonantal *u*) is English *w*: *virgō* = WIHR-goh.
- **i** before a vowel at the start of a word, and between vowels, is English
  *y*: *iam*, *iuvenis*. Between vowels it is doubled: *maior* = MAI-yor.
- **qu** is *kw*; **gu** after *n* is *gw*: *lingua* = LIHNG-gwah.
- **s** is always the *s* of *sing*, never *z*. **t** is always *t*: *grātia* = GRAH-tee-ah.
- **ph, th, ch** are *p, t, k* with a puff of breath, not *f, th, ch*.
- **gn** is *ngn*: *magnus* = MAHNG-noos. **n** before *c, g, q, x* is *ng*.
- **x** is *ks*. **z** (Greek words) is *dz* or *zz*.
- **h** is a light breath, often lost between vowels.
- Final **-m** after a vowel nasalises it, as in French: *caelum* = KAI-loom,
  IPA [ˈkae̯.ɫũː]. **n** before *s* or *f* does the same, lengthening the vowel.
- Double consonants are held: *an-nus*, *ec-ce*.
- **l** is "dark" (as in *full*) except before *i* and in *ll*; **r** is trilled.

Allen & Greenough cover the sounds in [§8](ag:8) and the alphabet in [§1](ag:1).

## Ecclesiastical

The Italianate pronunciation of the Roman Catholic Church: Latin read with the
sounds of Italian. Some such Italian-flavoured Latin is old; this standardised
form became the Church's official pronunciation in the early twentieth century,
and is what most choirs sing.

**Vowels** have no length contrast: *a* (ah), *e* (eh), *i* (ee), *o* (o),
*u* (oo). In the stressed syllable *e* and *o* are open, as in Italian.
**ae** and **oe** are plain *e*: *caelum* = CHEH-loom, *Phoebus* = FEH-boos.
**y** is *i*. **au** and **eu** stay diphthongs.

**Consonants** — what changes from Classical:

- **c** before *e, i, y, ae, oe* is *ch*: *Cicerō* = CHEE-cheh-ro. **cc** there
  is *t-ch*: *ecce* = EHT-cheh. Elsewhere *c* is *k*.
- **g** before *e, i, y, ae, oe* is *j*: *rēgīna* = reh-JEE-nah. Elsewhere hard.
- **sc** before those vowels is *sh*: *scelus* = SHEH-loos; **xc** is *ksh*:
  *excelsus* = ehk-SHEHL-soos.
- **gn** is *ny* (Italian *gnocchi*): *magnus* = MAHN-nyoos.
- **ti** before a vowel is *tsee*: *grātia* = GRAHT-tsee-ah — except after
  *s, t, x* (*hostia*).
- **v** is English *v*; **ph** is *f*; **th** is *t*; **ch** is *k*.
- **h** is silent — except in *mihi* and *nihil*, traditionally MEE-kee, NEE-keel.
- **ex-** before a vowel, when the accent follows, is *egz*: *exārdeō* = ehg-ZAHR-deh-o.
- **z** is *dz*. Final **-m** is a plain *m*.

## Syllables and accent

Both systems divide words and place the accent the same way, by the classical
rules ([A&G §7](ag:7), [§12](ag:12)).

A single consonant between vowels begins the next syllable (*a-mō*); of two or
more, the last begins it (*hor-tus*, *mag-nus*) — except that a stop followed
by *r* or *l* usually goes together (*pa-tris*). *x* counts as two consonants.

A syllable is **heavy** if its vowel is long or a diphthong, or if it ends in a
consonant. Then:

- Words of two syllables are stressed on the first: HOR-toos.
- Longer words are stressed on the second-to-last syllable if it is heavy
  (*a-MĪ-cus*, *ae-TER-nus*), and otherwise on the one before it
  (*DO-mi-nus*, *TE-ne-brae*).

This is why the macrons matter even in Ecclesiastical, which no longer
pronounces vowel length: *rēgīna* and *dominus* are both three syllables, but
the long *ī* pulls the accent forward in the first.

## Why Marvell opens in Ecclesiastical

No one in the seventeenth century spoke "Ecclesiastical Latin" in its modern,
standardised form, and Marvell left no account of how he pronounced Latin. The
default is an informed guess, and the switch is there to overrule it.

The case for it runs through Milton. In *Of Education* (1644) Milton told
schoolmasters that their pupils'

> speech is to be fashion'd to a distinct and clear pronuntiation, as near as
> may be to the Italian, especially in the Vowels. For we Englishmen being far
> Northerly, do not open our mouths in the cold air, wide enough to grace a
> Southern Tongue; but are observ'd by all other Nations to speak exceeding
> close and inward: So that to smatter Latine with an English mouth, is as ill
> a hearing as Law-French.

English schools then taught a native pronunciation of Latin that had followed
English through its vowel shifts — the "English mouth" Milton objected to.
Milton had lived in Italy in 1638–39 and wanted Latin spoken the Italian way.
Marvell was his close associate: Milton recommended him for government service
in 1653, praising his four years abroad "in Holland, France, Italy, and Spaine",
and in 1657 Marvell became Milton's assistant in the Latin Secretaryship.
Whether Marvell's Latin followed Milton's advice is unrecorded; it is
plausible, and the Italianate system is the nearest modern stand-in for
"as near as may be to the Italian".

Two cautions. Milton asks for Italian *vowels*; he says nothing about *c* and
*g*, and the consonants of seventeenth-century Latin varied from country to
country (in Venice, soft *c* was *ts*). And Marvell, schooled at Hull and
Cambridge, certainly *heard* the English pronunciation all his youth. Read the
Ecclesiastical popovers on *Hortus* as one plausible voice, not a recording.

## How the popovers work it out

The pronunciation is computed, not looked up. The texts are printed without
macrons, so the vowel lengths of each form come from its Wiktionary inflection
table (*mortale* in the text is *mortāle*: mor-TAH-leh); the reader then applies
the rules on this page. Checked against Wiktionary's own transcriptions of every
dictionary form in the lexicon, it agrees on about 99% of words in both systems.
The rest are mostly compounds whose prefix changes the syllables (*ad-stringō*),
and Greek names.

Where the text leaves a form's case open and the cases differ in length —
*vestra* (nominative) or *vestrā* (ablative) — the Classical line shows both;
the metre of the line usually decides. Where a form's lengths aren't known, the
popover pronounces the dictionary form instead and names it.
